Food · Ritual

The Long Table at Kilimani

Every Sunday for eleven years, the same forty people have eaten at the same table. Nobody planned it. We went to find out how a meal becomes an institution.

Words by A. Wanjiru Photographs to come Nairobi March 2026 12 min read

The table is not remarkable. It is three tables, actually, pushed end to end and covered with a cloth that does not quite reach the corners. It lives in a courtyard in Kilimani, under a jacaranda that drops flowers into the food for two weeks a year, and nobody has ever suggested moving it.

What is remarkable is that it has been set every Sunday since 2015. Not by an organisation. Not by a restaurant. By a woman who started cooking for four friends and never worked out how to stop.

How it started, which nobody agrees on

Ask five people at the table how the lunch began and you will get five accounts, all of them delivered with total confidence. One version has it starting as a birthday that overran. Another as a rota between three households that quietly collapsed into one kitchen. The host herself says she cannot remember, which everyone treats as modesty and which is probably just true.

What is not in dispute is the shape of it. Lunch is at two. It is never at two. You bring something or you do not, and nobody keeps a ledger, though everyone knows exactly who has not brought anything for a year.

Nobody was invited, so nobody can be uninvited. That is the whole constitution. A regular, on the rules

This is the part that interests us. The lunch has no membership, no mailing list and no name. It has survived two house moves, one divorce among its founders, a pandemic that shut it down for fourteen months, and the steady attrition of people leaving the city. It has survived, as far as we can tell, because it was never designed.

What gets cooked

The menu rotates without a rota. There is almost always a pilau, because it scales and because it forgives a late arrival. There is usually something green that somebody brought from their mother. On the Sundays after a good market week there is fish, and on the Sundays after a bad one there is more rice.

  • Lunch is called for two and served closer to four.
  • Children eat first, standing, and are not made to sit.
  • The last hour is the point; the food is the excuse.

Why it matters

It is tempting to write this up as hospitality, or as tradition, and both would be slightly wrong. Tradition implies inheritance and this was invented eleven years ago by people who are still alive to argue about it. Hospitality implies a host and a guest, and by the third or fourth Sunday that distinction has stopped meaning anything.

What it actually is, we think, is an ordinary thing done deliberately for long enough that it became infrastructure. Nobody set out to build an institution. They set out to have lunch, and then had it again.

That is not a small thing to have made. Most of what holds a city together looks like this from close up: unglamorous, repeated, and almost entirely undocumented.
