Most advice about mornings asks for more than a morning can hold. Rise at four. Journal three pages. Sit for twenty minutes, then cold water, then the gym, then the reading, then the day. It is a schedule that presumes nothing will go wrong, and mornings are precisely where things go wrong.

The rituals that last are the ones too small to fail. A glass of water. A walk. Kept in that order, every day, for years, through moves and illnesses and jobs that ended without warning.

## Water first, because it asks nothing of you

You wake dehydrated. Everyone does; eight hours of breathing sees to it. A pint of water before anything else rehydrates you and wakes the digestive system, and that is the whole of the physiological case. It is not a large claim.

The larger case is procedural. A ritual needs a first move that cannot be negotiated with, and water is unarguable. It costs nothing. It takes ninety seconds. It can be done while the kettle heats, while a child is being talked into a school uniform, while someone is telling you about their night. Nobody has ever been too tired to drink water.

What that first move buys is not hydration. It is the fact of having started.

## The walk has two halves, and they are not the same walk

Thirty minutes, roughly two kilometres, though the distance matters less than the time. What makes the walk work is that it is two things in sequence rather than one thing at length.

The first half is for the day ahead. Not planning exactly — planning wants paper — but rehearsal. What is coming. Which conversation is going to be difficult and why. Where the day is likely to go wrong, and what you will do at the point it does. Walking suits this better than sitting does. The problem stays in front of you instead of circling.

The second half is for none of that. You stop working the day over and simply walk. The light on the wall, the smell of the bakery, whoever is opening their shutters. This is as far as meditation needs to go for most people, and it is where the gratitude actually arrives — not summoned, but noticed, on a road you have walked four hundred times.

> The order matters more than the content. Think first, then stop thinking. A walk that is all rehearsal is just anxiety at four kilometres an hour.

## What a bad year does to a routine

Any routine will survive an ordinary week. The test is the year that arrives with a diagnosis in it, or a redundancy, or a small child and no help. That is when elaborate routines are abandoned entirely, and the abandonment is treated as a personal failure on top of everything else.

A small routine bends instead. The morning walk stays where it is and takes on a new job: walking through what the stress will feel like today and what you will do when it comes. Call it inhaling the day. A second walk appears at lunch, with something to listen to, because the middle of a hard day needs a seam in it. A third comes after dinner, once the house is quiet, with music and no thinking at all — exhaling the day, letting the thing that happened at eleven in the morning finally leave.

Three walks is more than one. But it is the same routine, extended along its own grain, not a new discipline adopted at the worst possible moment.

## The point is the structure, not the details

None of this is prescription. The water might be tea. The walk might be a staircase, or the length of a compound, or the road to the shop and back. What transfers is the shape: one unarguable first move, then a stretch of thinking, then a stretch of not thinking, in that order, at that hour, whatever the day turns out to contain.

A morning ritual does not make the day good. It makes the day begin the same way every time, which is a different and more useful thing. You still argue. You are still late. But the first twenty minutes belonged to you, and on the days when nothing else does, that turns out to be the whole of the benefit.

Ordinary living, made intentional, starts with a glass of water at ten past six.
